# County Road Q — Design Concept

Nov 22, 2026 · @Matthew Koski

## Pitch

You're a guy with a 1990s F-150 and a plow, working a Wisconsin blizzard that never ends. Your wife Deb runs the jobs from the kitchen table over the CB: driveways on contract, cars in ditches, people nobody has heard from. The work is calm and she's good company, until a voice that sounds exactly like her starts giving you directions too.

Working title: **Home Twenty** (CB talk for where home is). Tagline: *What's your twenty, hon?*

**No nights, no daylight.** One continuous storm with no day-night cycle and no dawn. Play is paced by how much of the township you've opened up. Snow you've cleared stays cleared however hard it comes down; that's video game logic, and the game never explains it.

**Why the late 1990s.** No cell phones or GPS. You have a CB, a paper plat book and a cassette deck; Deb has the base station and the other copy of the plat book. Five to seven hours long.

## Who plows what

In Wisconsin the county's heavy trucks handle county highways, while a local guy with a half-ton fits driveways, private roads and some town roads on contract.

- **County highways.** The county highway department, with heavy trucks.
- **Town roads.** Towns often hire private contractors to plow them.
- **Driveways.** State law lets towns and counties contract to plow private driveways, and many rural towns sell driveway contracts. Roads come first, except for ambulance and fire emergencies.
- **A half-ton pickup.** Fine for driveways and light work; commercial plowing calls for a 3/4- or 1-ton.

**In the game,** you hold the driveway contracts and a few town roads, plus whatever stretch “the county can't get to this storm.” You never see a county truck. The half-ton also gives you an upgrade path to a heavier truck or a borrowed tractor with a snowblower.

## The job

Deb's list is the spine of the game: small, concrete jobs you can lose yourself in.

- **The list.** Deb works from the kitchen table with the CB base station, the plat book and the town's contract list, and calls you with the next job.
- **Job types.** Clear a driveway on contract. Open a path to a propane tank so the delivery truck can fill it. Pull a car out of the ditch. Check on someone who isn't answering the phone. Open a road for an ambulance. Clear the lane to a barn.
- **The truck.** A 1990s F-150 4x4 with a light 7½-foot blade, sandbags in the bed for traction, and a tow chain behind the seat. Deep snow slows it to a crawl. Straighten the wheels to pull back up the shoulder, or reverse out of the drift.
- **No deadline.** Nothing is timed. The list grows, and Deb doesn't nag; she just adds.
- **Cleared stays cleared.** Once you plow a road or drive, it stays open while the snow everywhere else keeps deepening. Your plowed roads become a map of where you've been, and a sure way back.
- **Windrows and mailboxes.** Clean up the ridge your blade leaves across a drive and people notice. Knock down a mailbox and you can come back and fix it.
- **Home.** Your own farmhouse: the kitchen where Deb sits, the woodstove, the machine shed and a farm fuel tank. You can always stop in, and there's always coffee.

## Ask Deb

One button asks Deb for directions to the next contract from wherever you are. Nobody gets lost, and the button becomes the mystery's main channel.

- **How it plays.** You key the mic. She asks where you're at, the game fills in your nearest road and landmark, and she answers in two to four steps the way locals give directions: “Okay, you're by the co-op. Go north on K past the Lutheran church, and it's the second drive after the curve. Red barn.”
- **How it's built.** The roads are a graph. Route from your position, then turn the route into lefts and rights relative to your heading, road names, landmarks and rough distances. Lines can be stitched from recorded pieces, and the CB filter hides the seams.
- **Her voice.** She repeats herself if you ask twice, teases you the third time, and fills quiet stretches with town news and whatever she's reading.
- **When it breaks.** Off the map, she can't help: “Huh? Where are you? That's not on the map.” Getting back is on you, by landmarks and your own plowed roads.
- **The tell.** A CB can't receive while you're transmitting. If an answer starts while you're still holding the mic key, it isn't coming over the radio.

## Stopping

Stopping is the game's real verb. It is never punished, and it's the main way you get stronger.

**Mundane stops**, common from the start:

- Pull a car out of the ditch with a tow chain, or jump a dead battery.
- Mrs. Ostrowski's furnace is out: go down to her basement and relight the pilot.
- Cattle or a dog loose on the road.
- A stranded driver at the closed gas station: coffee from your thermos and a call to Deb.
- Fix the mailbox you knocked down.

**Uncanny stops**, rare at first and more common as you open the township:

- Someone walking the road asks for a ride, then says nothing. In the mirror the passenger looks like Deb; when you stop, the seat is empty and wet.
- A payphone ringing at the closed gas station. It's Deb, asking why you aren't answering the radio.
- A porch light on over a foundation with no house.
- A lit farmhouse at the end of a road that isn't in your plat book.
- A figure far out in a field, facing away from the road toward something you can't see.

**Gifts are progression.** People you help give you things that make the cold easier: a knit hat, a thermos refill, a tow strap, a shortcut through a field road, a key. Your gear improves through kindness, so the game teaches you to stop.

## The cold

The cab is safe and warm. Outside, the cold is the only physical threat and the source of most of the urgency.

- **Warmth you see, not a meter.** Your breath fogs, frost creeps in from the screen edges, and your hands shake, which makes chains, pilot lights and latches harder.
- **What drains it.** Wind and wet clothes. The shelter of a building buys time.
- **What restores it.** The cab heater, a kitchen with the stove going, and a thermos with a few pours.
- **Whiteout.** In a gust you can lose sight of the truck within twenty steps. The amber strobe and the horn are your lighthouse.
- **The rope line.** An early upgrade: rope you tie off at the truck and pay out as you walk, the way farm families once strung lines between house and barn in blizzards.
- **Fuel.** An empty tank means no heater.
- **Collapse is not death.** Go down in the cold and you wake in your cab with the door shut and the heater running. A set of boot prints leads away, toward the old road. You lose time, and the mystery gains a thread.

## Finding your way

There's no GPS or minimap, but Ask Deb means you're never stuck.

- **The plat book.** Your copy rides on the dash and Deb has the other. Each shows the landowner's name on every parcel, and you mark yours up: houses, hazards, people.
- **The survey grid.** Roads follow the one-mile section grid, so you navigate by counting crossings. Anything that runs at a slant across the grid is wrong.
- **Road edges.** A drainage ditch runs down each shoulder and opens at drives and crossroads. When the fields disappear into blowing snow, you can still follow those two lines in the headlights.
- **Landmarks in the fog.** You can see 50–150 meters, so landmarks are silhouettes: silos, steeples, the feed mill, water towers, ginseng shade frames.
- **Your own plowed roads.** Cleared stays cleared, so the roads you've opened are always a trail back to somewhere you know.

## Progression

Five things make you stronger, and none of them is story.

| Source | How you earn it | What it unlocks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gifts from people | Stopping to help | Warmth gear, thermos refills, a tow strap, shortcuts, keys |
| Contract pay | Working the list | Truck and kit: rope line, tire chains, a bigger blade, a light bar, a better heater, eventually a 3/4-ton truck |
| Borrowed equipment | Favors from farmers | A tractor with a snowblower for snow the truck can't move |
| Knowledge | Driving, looking, marking up your plat book | Faster routes and knowing where people are |
| The mystery | Following what's wrong | Access to the places the voices keep pointing at |

## The mystery

Spoilers. Something on a road older than the map calls people out into the storm in the voice of someone they love, and this time it wants your house.

**The old road.** Before the section-line roads, the Pinery Road cut across the township at a slant, hauling for the logging camps. The town vacated it generations ago, and no plat book since shows it. In the big snows it comes back.

**The rider.** Old-timers have a few names for what lives on it and don't say any of them out loud. It takes any shape: a walker who wants a ride, a dog in the road, a neighbor at the door. In a big snow it calls people out of their houses in the voice of someone they love, and they follow it down the old road to a house that isn't on the map. It borrows from three pieces of Wisconsin lore: the Ridgeway Ghost, the shape-changing phantom of an old road; the hidebehind, the lumber-camp creature blamed for men who never came back; and Bloody Bride Bridge, where a woman appears in drivers' back seats.

**The pattern.** The big snows come about every 40 years: 1878, 1918, 1958 and now. The town clerk's ledger shows each storm ending the same night someone in the township went missing, in entries as flat and terrible as Wisconsin Death Trip. The cemetery holds empty graves from each of those winters. The real Ridgeway Ghost was also said to return in roughly 40-year cycles.

**Why this storm won't end.** Phones and CBs mean people check on each other now, and Deb's list means someone looks in on every house. Nobody follows the voice, so the storm waits. Your kindness keeps the township safe and keeps the storm going.

**So it turns to you.** You and Deb are the ones doing the checking. It learns her voice from the channel, starts answering your Ask Deb calls, and finally learns yours.

**It keeps you alive.** When you collapse in the cold, it's the one that carries you back to your cab. It wants you to come home, not freeze in a ditch.

## How it escalates

Paced by how much of the township you've opened, not by nights.

1. **The first sections.** Learn the list, Deb and Ask Deb; her directions are always right. At the tavern, old Ed Halvorsen: “Don't pick up anybody walking in a big snow. And don't go when home calls.”
2. **Crossed wires.** Deb calls a job you can't find. When you ask again, she says she never called it. Someone rides in your back seat, visible only in the mirror.
3. **The slant.** Following those directions, you end up on a road cutting across the grid at an angle. Ask Deb: “Huh? Where are you? That's not on the map.” At its end is a lit farmhouse, a family glad to see you, and a calendar from 1958.
4. **The pattern.** Deb sends you to plow the town hall lot. Inside is the clerk's ledger: 1878, 1918, 1958, each storm ending with a name. Behind the church are the empty graves.
5. **Two Debs.** You ask for directions and get two answers, one of them starting while you're still holding the key. You stop by the house and she says, “Didn't you just call? You said you were pulling in.”
6. **Your voice.** Deb on the channel: “You told me to meet you at the end of the drive. I've got my coat on.” You never said it.

## Endings

There's no dawn in either. The storm ends for the township, never for you.

**Main ending: Almost home.** You can't get through to Deb, because the thing is all over the channel, and you know how these storms end. You turn onto the old road and follow the voice's directions to a house that isn't on the map: kitchen light on, coffee on the stove, a woman at the table in Deb's sweater with her back to you. On the radio, the real Deb, faint and far away: “Hon? Where are you? That's not on the map.” The radio on this kitchen table answers her in your voice: “Almost home.”

**Alternative: She goes.** You drive home instead and find the coat hook empty and the CB on. Deb's voice comes over it from somewhere out on the old road: “Hon? Where are you?” You take her chair at the base station, and the game ends with you giving directions to someone you can't reach.

**After the credits.** A free-plow mode keeps the list going with no mystery, for as long as you like.

## Look and sound

The target is PS2-era Silent Hill 2: thick fog, heavy film grain, glowing lights, and a world that never quite comes into focus.

- **Storm light, never daylight.** There is no sun, ever. A flat gray-white glow from the snow and low cloud lets you see 50–150 meters without squinting.
- **No shadows.** The light comes from nowhere, so nothing casts a shadow except under your own headlights, flashlight and strobe.
- **Fog and snow as one system.** Visibility tightens in gusts and opens in lulls, which also sets how far landmarks read.
- **Grain and color.** A constant noise filter, slightly washed-out color, and a glow around every warm light: porch lights, kitchen windows, the radio's dial.
- **People.** More detailed faces than the PS1 era, held a little too still, with lines that land a beat late.
- **Sound.** Wind, the blade scraping, the heater fan and CB static, over low drones you can't quite separate from the wind. Deb's voice is the one warm, clear sound in the game.
- **The old road looks older.** On the slant the image drifts toward faded 1950s color film, and the truck radio finds only one station, playing music from 1958.

## Building from real maps

The township can sit on real Wisconsin terrain and roads, with fictional names on top.

- **Terrain.** Lidar elevation is available for all 72 Wisconsin counties through GeoData@Wisconsin.
- **Roads and parcels.** Trace roads from OpenStreetMap and the section grid, and build the in-game plat book from real parcel shapes with invented owners. The same road graph drives Ask Deb.
- **The old road.** Early wagon and logging roads followed the land rather than the grid. Old county maps can suggest where a slanted road might have run before the grid replaced it.
- **Scale.** A survey township is six miles square. Start with about two by three miles, so the truck crosses it in a few minutes and every landmark gets attention.
- **Real places, fictional people.** Rename families, farms and businesses. The folklore is borrowed in shape; the township and its disappearances are invented.

## First prototype

Build the smallest thing that answers one question: is plowing, stepping out into the cold, and climbing back into a warm cab fun for twenty minutes on its own?

1. The F-150 and blade on a one-square-mile chunk of real lidar terrain.
2. Snow as a height map the blade carves, staying cleared once plowed.
3. Getting out of the truck: warmth, whiteout, strobe and horn.
4. Ask Deb on a small road graph, with three jobs: a driveway, a car in a ditch, a welfare check.
5. One collapse that wakes you in the cab, with boot prints leading away.
6. The look: fog, grain, glowing lights and shadowless storm light.

Leave out the mystery and progression for now. If the loop isn't fun on its own, the mystery won't save it.
